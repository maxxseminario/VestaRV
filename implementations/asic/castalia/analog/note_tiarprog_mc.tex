Corners move every code by the same sheet-resistance factor, so matching ---
the property that decides whether a 64-unit thermometer segment can invert a
step against 63 binary units --- is invisible to them. The Monte Carlo run
therefore grades the seam directly, per sample, alongside the absolute spread
at four codes.

Every graded code carries a \(\pm 5\,\%\) absolute window about its typical
value, and the seam a monotonicity limit at zero; all five yield 100.0\,\% over
the 200 samples (Table~\ref{tab:tiarprog-mc}). The margins are not close: the
nearest limit sits \(14.2\sigma\) from the mean at \(N{=}0\), \(54\sigma\) at
\(N{=}63\), \(55\sigma\) at \(N{=}64\) and \(134\sigma\) at \(N{=}319\), and
the worst seam sample is \(14.7\sigma\) above zero. Mismatch is therefore
nowhere near yield-limiting for this block; the items that need engineering
attention are the deterministic ones graded in
Table~\ref{tab:tiarprog-spec} --- bottom-code linearity and slow-corner
top-code compliance.

\paragraph{All four seams, all 320 codes.} A second 200-sample run sweeps the
whole code space in every sample, so each sample is graded at all four
thermometer seams and none can contribute to one seam and not another
(Table~\ref{tab:tiarprog-seams}). \textbf{No sample is non-monotonic anywhere:}
zero codes with a negative step, in 200 samples across 320 codes. The nearest
approach to zero is not a seam but the smallest ordinary step,
\SI{5470}{\ohm} in the worst sample, \(137\sigma\) from zero on its own
distribution; the four seam steps clear zero by \numrange{14.7}{17.7} standard
deviations. The worst of them is the first, and predictably so: it is the only
seam at which a thermometer segment switches in with no thermometer segment
already in circuit, so its step is the smallest and its relative spread the
largest.

The ratio the seams rest on measures well. Sixty-four thermometer units against
sixty-three binary units read \num{1.0197} against an ideal \num{1.015873} ---
\SI{0.38}{\percent} high, which is switch resistance and does not cancel between
the two differences --- with a spread of \SI{0.132}{\percent}, i.e.
\SI{0.13}{\percent} on one thermometer segment. It is the spread and not the
offset that decides monotonicity, and the offset calibrates out.
